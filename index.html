Lecture 1: Principles of Radiology Interpretations

Foundational Principles and Objectives of Radiographic Interpretation

Concept Key Details
Interpretation vs. Diagnosis Interpretation = explanation of what is viewed on a dental radiograph; the ability to read what is revealed. Diagnosis = identification of disease by examination or analysis.
Objectives of Radiographic Interpretation
  1. Detection – presence or absence of disease.
  2. Description – providing information on disease according to a systematic approach.
  3. Differentiation – distinguishing diseases of similar radiographic appearance.
Importance of Radiologic Interpretation Dentists must master two inseparable components of visual diagnosis:
  • Perception – recognizing abnormal patterns.
  • Cognition – interpreting those patterns.
Clinical Examination and Radiograph Prescription Radiographs are prescribed when they offer useful diagnostic information. Clinical signs, symptoms, and history indicate the need for a radiological examination and should first be used to select the type of radiographs.
Acquiring Appropriate Diagnostic Images An insufficient number or inadequate quality of radiographs limits diagnostic information. Inadequate radiographs must be recognized, and supplemental images obtained before analysis.
Essential Requirements for Interpretation
  • Optimum viewing conditions.
  • Knowledge of expected radiographic appearance.
  • Knowledge of normal anatomical landmarks and variations of normal.
  • Knowledge of radiographic appearances of pathological maxillofacial conditions.
Quality of Diagnostic X-rays Conventional dental radiography produces 2-dimensional images, usually mesiodistal. A view at right angles to the original film plane is sometimes beneficial. Complete diagnostic imaging before biopsy or treatment; it can help select an appropriate biopsy site.
Image Analysis – Systematic Approach First identify all normal anatomy. Learn normal anatomy by identifying it in every image. Examine every aspect systematically rather than focusing on one region; more than one abnormality may be present.
Intraoral Imaging Periapical and bitewing radiographs evaluate the crown, root, pulp, PDL space, and lamina dura. Examine both jaws, bone quality and trabecular pattern, teeth, and restorations.
Extraoral Radiography Common dental extraoral studies include panoramic images, cephalometric views, and TMJ examinations. For panoramic images, review maxillary border, sinus floor, and zygomatic process. For CBCT, review axial, coronal, and sagittal planes.
Importance of Normal Anatomy Knowledge Recognizing normal variations is key to identifying abnormalities, especially on panoramic images.
Diagnostic Reasoning Process Differentiate normal from abnormal in 2-dimensional images, then form a 3-dimensional mental representation including location, size, internal structure, and effects on surrounding structures.
Ineffective vs. Effective Strategy Ineffective: Memorizing abnormality features without understanding mechanisms. Effective: Understanding pathophysiology at biochemical, cellular, tissue, and organ levels.
Worth’s Principle Radiographic appearances are governed by anatomic and physiologic changes in disease processes. Radiologic diagnosis requires knowledge of these alterations and disease mechanisms.

Systematic Analysis of Radiographic Abnormalities

Step 1: Localise the Lesion

Aspect Details
Localised or Generalised? Many abnormalities are localised. If all osseous structures of the maxillofacial region are affected, consider generalised conditions such as metabolic or endocrine bone abnormalities.
Unilateral or Bilateral? Variations of normal are more commonly bilateral; abnormalities are more commonly unilateral. Exceptions include Paget’s disease and cherubism, which are bilateral in the mandible.
Position in the Jaws (Epicenter) The point of origin suggests tissue of origin:
  • Coronal to a tooth: likely odontogenic epithelium.
  • Above the inferior alveolar nerve canal: likely odontogenic tissue.
  • Below the inferior alveolar nerve canal: unlikely odontogenic, such as a Stafne defect.
  • Within the inferior alveolar nerve canal: likely neural or vascular origin.
  • Condylar region: greater likelihood of cartilaginous lesions or osteochondromas.
  • Within maxillary antrum: not odontogenic unless it grew into the antrum from the alveolar process.
Specific Location Predilections
  • Central giant cell granuloma: anterior to first molars in the mandible and anterior to the canine in the maxilla, usually in young patients.
  • Osteomyelitis: usually mandible, rarely maxilla.
  • Periapical cemental dysplasia: periapical tooth region.
Single or Multifocal? Possible multifocal abnormalities include periapical cemental dysplasia, odontogenic keratocyst, metastatic lesions, multiple myeloma, and leukemic infiltrates.
Size Size may aid differential diagnosis; for example, a dentigerous cyst is often larger than a hyperplastic follicle.

Step 2: Assess the Periphery and Shape

Feature Description
Well-defined border An imaginary pencil can confidently trace lesion limits. Suggests a slow-growing or benign process.
Punched-out border Sharp boundary with no adjacent bone reaction. Seen in multiple myeloma.
Corticated margin Thin, fairly uniform radiopaque line of reactive bone at the periphery. Seen in cysts.
Sclerotic margin Wider radiopaque reactive border, usually uneven in width. Seen in periapical cemental dysplasia.
Soft tissue capsule A radiopaque lesion may have a radiolucent peripheral line indicating a soft-tissue capsule. Seen in odontomas and cementoblastomas.
Ill-defined border – Blending Gradual transition between normal and abnormal trabeculae. Seen in sclerosing osteitis and fibrous dysplasia.
Ill-defined border – Invasive Associated with rapid growth and malignant lesions. Radiolucent finger-like or bay-type extensions may occur at the periphery with bone destruction.
Shape
  • Circular: fluid-filled, hydraulic lesions such as cysts.
  • Scalloped: contiguous arcs or semicircles; seen in odontogenic keratocysts, simple bone cysts, and some tumours.
  • Other: irregular, inverted pear, or heart-shaped.

Step 3: Analyse the Internal Structure

Category Examples and Patterns
Totally radiolucent Common in cysts.
Totally radiopaque Observed in osteomas.
Mixed radiolucent-radiopaque Calcified structures against a radiolucent backdrop.
Fibrous dysplasia Greater numbers of short, randomly oriented trabeculae; orange-peel or ground-glass appearance.
Septa Residual trapped bone forms strands or walls. Septa dividing a lesion into at least two compartments indicate multilocularity.
  • Ameloblastoma: curved septa and soap-bubble compartments.
  • Central giant cell granuloma: wispy or granular faint septations.
  • Odontogenic myxoma: straight thin septa.
Dystrophic calcification Occurs in damaged soft tissue. Calcified lymph nodes appear as dense, cauliflower-like soft-tissue masses.
Cementum Homogeneous, dense, amorphous structure, sometimes organized in round or oval forms.
Tooth structure Recognized by organization into enamel, dentin, and pulp chambers; density exceeds surrounding bone.
Relative radiodensity Least to most radiopaque:
  1. Air, fat, or gas
  2. Fluid
  3. Soft tissue
  4. Bone marrow
  5. Trabecular bone
  6. Cortical bone or dentin
  7. Enamel
  8. Metal

    Step 5: Formulate a Radiographic Interpretation

    Decision Key Questions and Guidance
    Normal or Abnormal? Decide whether the finding is a variation of normal or an abnormality. Variations of normal do not require treatment.
    Developmental or Acquired? Determine whether the finding developed during formation or later. A short root may be developmental when the root canal is not visible to the apex, versus external resorption when the canal is visible to the apex and pulp chambers are wide at the apex.
    Disease Classification Identify the most likely category: inflammation, cyst, benign or malignant neoplasia, bone dysplasia, vascular or metabolic disease, or trauma.
    How to Proceed Possible next steps include further imaging, biopsy, observation, or treatment. Suspected malignancy requires advanced imaging and biopsy; some benign lesions may be observed.
    Integrating Clinical Information Use clinical information after narrowing radiographic possibilities to avoid bias and refine the differential diagnosis.

    Diagnostic Imaging Report Structure

    Section Content
    General Patient Information Patient name, age, sex, patient ID or registration number, referring clinician if applicable, and report date.
    Imaging Procedure Summary of procedures performed and examination date. For CBCT, include field of view, slice thickness, spacing, and image quality.
    Clinical Information Optional brief summary of clinical findings and history supplied by the referring clinician or examiner.
    Findings Objective observations only: anatomic location, lesion extent, periphery and shape, internal structure, and effects on surrounding structures. Do not include interpretation.
    Interpretation Explain the meaning of the findings, integrate clinical history if available, and provide a diagnosis or limited differential diagnosis.
    Recommendations Suggest further studies or treatment when applicable, such as biopsy or follow-up imaging.
    Report Finalization Include the reporting clinician’s name and signature. Ensure clarity, completeness, and a standardized professional format.

    Lecture 2: Dental Caries and Periodontal Diseases

    Dental Caries & Periodontal Diseases — Comprehensive Study Notes

    Dental Caries — Disease Mechanism

    Aspect Details
    Etiology Multifactorial: involves the tooth, dental biofilm, and diet; influenced by saliva, fluoride exposure, immune system, bacterial exposure duration, and socioeconomic status.
    Role of Streptococcus mutans Ferments dietary sugars, especially fructose, producing lactic acid that lowers pH and causes demineralization.
    Demineralization process Begins beneath the enamel surface and leaves a thin intact surface layer. It involves cycles of demineralization and remineralization.
    Modifying the cycle
    • Reduce sugar intake.
    • Improve oral hygiene.
    • Use topical fluoride.
    Clinical presentation
    • Early lesion: white, chalky appearance.
    • Arrested lesion: dark brown or black appearance.
    Key rate difference Dentin demineralizes faster than enamel.
    Progression endpoint Can lead to pulp necrosis.

    Role of Imaging in Caries Detection

    Topic Details
    Clinical limitations Occlusal and smooth-surface caries may be visible; interproximal caries are often missed unless cavitated.
    Preferred imaging for interproximal caries Intraoral bitewing radiographs.
    Panoramic images Low resolution; not ideal for caries detection.
    Radiographic appearance Demineralized areas are radiolucent. A single image cannot distinguish active from arrested lesions.
    Extraoral bitewings An alternative for gag reflex, disabilities, or anatomical constraints, but less reliable than intraoral bitewings.

    Imaging Intervals by Caries Risk

    Risk Level Children Adolescents Adults
    High risk 6–12 months — 6–18 months
    Low risk 12–24 months 18–36 months 24–36 months

    Intraoral Imaging — Digital Sensors vs. Film

    Modality Types Advantages Challenges / Cautions
    Digital sensors Solid-state (CMOS); photostimulable phosphor plates (PSP). Immediate acquisition; sensitivity equivalent to film for most caries; PSP is similar in size to film and easier to place. Solid-state sensors may be bulky and uncomfortable; smaller active area may require multiple images; use a high-quality monitor and subdued lighting.
    Intraoral film ANSI size 2 for adolescents/adults; ANSI size 0 or 1 for children. Familiar technique. Mount with dark borders; view on a light box with magnification and subdued lighting. Avoid ANSI size 3 film because bending causes distortion.

    Caries Detection by Surface Type

    Proximal Surfaces

    Feature Details
    Enamel caries shape Classic triangular radiolucency with base at the surface and tip at the DEJ; may also appear as a band, rectangle, or semicircular notch.
    Dentin involvement Second triangle with base at the DEJ and tip toward pulp; more aggressive because dentin has lower mineral content.
    Common location Between contact points to near the gingival margin.
    Critical distinction Must be differentiated from cervical burnout.
    Radiographic limitation About 35% of mineral content must be lost for visibility; true depth is greater than the radiographic appearance.

    Occlusal Surfaces

    Feature Details
    Population affected Common in children and adolescents.
    Radiographic detection Difficult in enamel; usually visible after reaching dentin as a semicircular shape at the DEJ.
    Clinical signs New lesions are chalky white; older lesions may be brown or black.
    Role of imaging Assesses depth rather than early detection.
    False negatives Common because of enamel superimposition.
    Mach-band effect Optical illusion at the DEJ from enamel-dentin contrast. Mask enamel: if the band disappears, no caries; if it remains, caries is more likely. Monitor suspicious areas without clinical signs.

    Buccal, Lingual, and Root Surface Caries

    Feature Details
    Buccal/lingual caries Circular or oval and well-defined; use tube-shift technique to localize on radiographs.
    Root caries Common with gingival recession or bone loss; thin cementum permits rapid progression.
    Differentiation Differentiate from cervical burnout with clinical examination and bone-level assessment.

    Cervical Burnout vs. Cervical Caries

    Feature Cervical Burnout Cervical Caries
    Appearance Diffuse radiolucent band near the CEJ. Well-defined radiolucent lesion.
    Borders Gradual, less-defined edges. Sharp, irregular borders.
    Root outline Remains visible. May be obscured.
    Associated conditions Normal bone height. Often bone loss or gingival recession.
    Effect of angulation Changes with horizontal angulation. Remains consistent with angulation.
    Causes Thin root structure at CEJ, lack of bone coverage, and X-ray attenuation differences. —
    Common in Single-rooted teeth with concavities and multi-rooted teeth with root overlap. —
    Avoiding misdiagnosis Identify PDL spaces, adjust horizontal angulation, and use clinical experience. —

    False-Positive Interpretations

    Aspect Details
    Observer variability Less than 100% agreement in caries diagnosis.
    Common causes Cervical burnout, overlapping proximal surfaces, and inexperience.
    Prevention Proper training, correct X-ray beam angulation, and high-quality images.

    Caries Staging — ICDAS/ICCMS System

    Stage Category Radiographic Criteria
    0 Sound surface No caries.
    RA (RA1–RA3) Initial stage RA1: outer enamel; RA2: inner enamel ± DEJ; RA3: outer third of dentin.
    RB (RB4) Moderate stage Middle third of dentin.
    RC (RC5–RC6) Extensive stage Inner third or pulp with cavitation.

    Treatment Considerations

    Aspect Details
    Determinants Caries risk status, lesion depth, and cavitation.
    Non-surgical management For ICCMS RA stage lesions in enamel or outer third of dentin: oral hygiene instruction and topical fluoride.
    Surgical management For cavitated lesions and lesions in the middle third of dentin (RB stage) or deeper.
    Follow-up strategy Schedule imaging by caries risk. Match new images to original images; progression indicates activity and may require surgery.
    Global treatment framework
    • Enamel-only: fluoride, hygiene, and diet modification.
    • Early dentin: may or may not need restoration.
    • Deep dentin or cavitated: surgical removal and restoration.

    Rampant Caries and Restorative Caries

    Rampant Caries

    Population Causes Characteristics Prevention
    Children Poor hygiene; sugary drinks, including bedtime bottles. Rapid widespread decay on smooth surfaces near cervical areas. Fluoride and saliva substitutes.
    Adults Xerostomia, including after radiation therapy. Rapid widespread decay. Fluoride and saliva substitutes.

    Caries Associated with Dental Restorations

    Type Definition Radiographic Appearance / Notes
    Recurrent (secondary) caries New demineralization at a restoration margin. Due to defective restorations or poor hygiene; radiolucency at restoration-tooth interface.
    Residual caries Unremoved carious tissue left intentionally or unintentionally. Often part of an indirect pulp-capping strategy.
    Imaging challenges Improper angulation may mask lesions. Use bitewings with correct vertical angulation; consider a second angle.
    Material confusion Radiolucent liners or bases may mimic caries. Older composites can appear carious but have well-defined borders.

    Alternative Diagnostic Tools

    Tool Application
    Light-induced fluorescence Detects demineralization on smooth surfaces.
    Lasers, transillumination, electrical conductance Used for occlusal and interproximal surfaces.
    AI in dentistry Can enhance accuracy but should complement, not replace, clinical judgment.

    Periodontal Diseases — Overview and Mechanism

    Aspect Details
    Periodontium Gingiva, periodontal ligament (PDL), cementum, and alveolar bone.
    Definition Inflammatory conditions affecting tooth-supporting structures.
    Consequence if untreated Bone loss, soft-tissue damage, and tooth loss.
    Prevalence About 42% of U.S. adults aged 30–79 have periodontitis.
    Risk groups Older adults and lower socioeconomic groups.
    Initiation Dental plaque biofilm.
    Key bacteria Gram-negative rods and spirochetes.
    Pathogenesis Bacterial toxins and host inflammatory response cause tissue destruction, bone resorption, and pocket formation.
    Disease progression Occurs in bursts with active and quiescent phases.
    Modifying factors Age, stress, smoking, hormones, and systemic disease.

    Clinical Signs

    Condition Signs
    Gingivitis Swelling, redness, and bleeding.
    Periodontitis Pocket formation, gingival recession, clinical attachment loss, tooth mobility, bleeding on probing, and pus.
    Key note Often painless, so patients may be unaware until advanced stages.

    Radiographic Assessment of Periodontal Conditions

    Features Evaluated

    Category Specific Features
    Bone assessment Amount of bone, alveolar crests, furcation bone loss, and PDL-space width.
    Local irritating factors Calculus and poorly contoured or overextended restorations.
    Root assessment Root length, morphology, and crown-to-root ratio.
    Interproximal factors Open contacts that allow food impaction.
    Anatomic considerations Root proximity; maxillary sinus and mental foramen position relative to periodontal defects.

    Imaging Modalities

    Modality Role / Characteristics
    Intraoral imaging Highest spatial resolution for subtle PDL, lamina dura, and early bone-loss changes.
    Bitewings Best for CEJ-to-alveolar crest distance; use vertical bitewings with significant bone loss.
    Periapical images Show full tooth length but may distort CEJ-crest relationship, especially in the maxilla.
    Panoramic imaging Quick full-jaw overview, but lower resolution, distortion, and superimposition; not primary for periodontal diagnosis.
    Extraoral bitewings Available on some panoramic systems, but evidence does not support periodontal assessment use.

    Limitations of Intraoral Imaging

    • 2D imaging does not clearly show buccal or lingual defects.
    • Superimposition may obscure crestal bone.
    • Bone loss is underestimated.
    • Soft tissue and pocket depth cannot be assessed.
    • The CEJ is not a reliable reference in supraeruption or attrition.

    CBCT in Periodontal Assessment

    Aspect Details
    Advantages 3D osseous visualization without superimposition; improved evaluation of vertical defects, furcations, buccal/lingual bone loss, root morphology, fenestrations, and periapical disease.
    Limitations Metal artifacts, higher cost, radiation dose, and time; not routinely recommended.
    Indications Small-volume, high-resolution CBCT for surgical planning or complex bony lesions.

    Normal Radiographic Anatomy of the Periodontium

    Structure Normal Appearance
    Alveolar crest Radiopaque cortical bone 0.5–2.0 mm apical to the CEJ.
    Posterior teeth Crest parallels the CEJ line.
    Anterior teeth Crest appears peak-like.
    PDL space Thin and uniform; may widen near the cervical root in youth.
    Variations Thin crests or radiolucency may be normal.

    Radiographic Features of Periodontal Disease

    Condition Radiographic Features
    Gingivitis No radiographic bone changes.
    Periodontitis: morphologic changes Loss of interproximal bone and cortical plate.
    Periodontitis: trabecular changes Rarefaction increases radiolucency; sclerosis increases radiopacity.
    Acute vs. chronic Acute disease causes bone loss; chronic disease may show sclerosis plus loss.

    Early and Horizontal Bone Loss

    Pattern Features
    Early signs Blunting of anterior crests, rounding between posterior crest and lamina dura, and diffuse cortical edge.
    Horizontal bone loss Bone crest remains parallel to the CEJ line; severity is based on root-length relationship.
    Severity Bone Level Relative to Root Length
    Mild Within coronal 15%.
    Moderate 15–33%.
    Severe Beyond 33%.

    Advanced Bone Loss Patterns

    Pattern Description Key Radiographic Signs
    Vertical (angular) defects V-shaped bone loss along root surface. Oblique to CEJ line; early widened PDL at crest; classified by remaining bony walls.
    Interdental craters Two-walled defects between adjacent teeth. Band-like crestal radiolucency; common in posterior jaws.
    Cortical plate loss Loss of buccal or lingual cortical plate. Semicircular radiolucency near crest; difficult to detect without interproximal loss.
    Furcation involvement Bone loss into molar furcations. More visible in mandibular molars; palatal root may obscure maxillary defects. Widened furcation PDL or radiolucent triangle/inverted J-shape may be present.
    Vertical Defect Type Description
    3-walled Buccal and lingual plates intact.
    2-walled One plate lost.
    1-walled Both plates lost.

    Gutta-percha tracing or CBCT may aid visualization. CBCT is not routine and is reserved for complex cases.

    Periodontal attachment loss can only be evaluated clinically.

    Bone Density and Trabecular Pattern Changes

    Change Type Features
    Radiolucent changes Loss of trabecular density; faint or invisible trabeculae in early or acute inflammation; may remineralize after successful treatment.
    Radiopaque changes Thickened trabeculae and increased radiopacity; may become amorphous in severe cases; sclerosis may extend to mandibular cortex or sinus floor.
    Other findings Maxillary sinus mucositis from inflammatory spread; rare periosteal reactions in adjacent bone.

    Feature Stage I Stage II Stage III Stage IV
    Radiographic bone loss Coronal third of root, less than 15%. Coronal third of root, 15–33%. Middle third of root and beyond. Middle third of root and beyond.
    Complexity Local. Maximum probing depth 4 mm or less; mostly horizontal loss. Maximum probing depth 5 mm or less; mostly horizontal loss. Stage II complexity and probing depth 6 mm or more; vertical bone loss 3 mm or more; Class II/III furcation; moderate ridge defect.
    Parameter Options
    Extent and distribution Localized, generalized, or molar-incisor distribution.
    Grades Grade A: slow; Grade B: moderate; Grade C: rapid progression.

    Necrotizing and Systemic Disease-Associated Periodontal Diseases

    Condition Key Features
    Necrotizing periodontal diseases Severe pain, fever, ulceration, and necrosis. Risk factors include HIV, malnutrition, stress, and smoking.
    Systemic disease-associated periodontitis Associated with leukemia, neutropenia, Down syndrome, and Papillon-Lefèvre syndrome; often related to immune dysfunction or connective-tissue defects.

    Other Conditions Affecting the Periodontium

    Periodontal Abscess

    Feature Details
    Acute phase Radiographic features may be absent.
    Chronic abscess Radiolucency superimposed over root, loss of lamina dura at root surface, and possible bone bridge separating lesion from alveolar crest.
    Healing Bone may regenerate after treatment.

    Endodontic-Periodontic Lesions

    Origin Pathway
    Periodontal origin Periodontal lesion extends to apex, causing secondary pulpitis.
    Endodontic origin Periapical lesion extends coronally, causing retrograde periodontitis.
    Radiographic appearance Deep angular defect extending to apex, possible periapical rarefying osteitis, and funnel-shaped bone loss.
    Treatment Both endodontic and periodontal therapy are required.

    Occlusal Trauma, Tooth Mobility, and Local Factors

    Condition Imaging Signs
    Occlusal trauma Widened PDL, thickened lamina dura, bone loss, and hypercementosis. It does not cause periodontitis but may accelerate bone loss if disease is present.
    Tooth mobility in single-rooted teeth Hourglass-shaped socket; widened PDL at apex and furcation in multi-rooted teeth; lamina dura may be broad and dense.
    Factor Consequence
    Open contacts Food impaction, localized inflammation, and bone loss.
    Malalignment Tipped teeth and uneven marginal ridges do not directly cause disease but hinder hygiene and promote plaque accumulation.
    Local Irritating Factor Details
    Calculus Promotes plaque retention; common near mandibular incisors.
    Defective restorations Overhangs and poor contours trap plaque.
    Enamel pearls and cervical enamel projections Common in furcations and disrupt attachment.

    Evaluation of Therapy and Differential Diagnosis

    Post-Treatment Imaging

    Aspect Details
    Signs of healing Recortication, sclerosis, and remineralization.
    Caveats Beam angulation and exposure affect interpretation. No visible change does not mean no healing; clinical examination remains essential.

    Differential Diagnosis of Bone Loss

    Aspect Periodontal Bone Loss Red Flags for Non-Periodontal Disease
    Pattern Starts at alveolar crest and progresses apically; wider coronally and tapering apically. Isolated tooth mobility or irregular loss that does not follow periodontal patterns.
    Borders — Ill-defined borders and no sclerosis.
    Other pathology — Squamous cell carcinoma: irregular PDL widening and lamina dura destruction. Langerhans cell histiocytosis: floating teeth and mid-root epicenter.
